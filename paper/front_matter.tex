\begin{abstract}
Bengali is spoken by more than 230 million people and remains thinly
represented in language model pretraining. Two recent efforts respond to this
by pretraining smaller models heavily on Bengali, on the implicit premise that
targeted language depth can stand in for parameter scale. We test that premise
directly. Seven backbones from 0.6B to 4B parameters are compared on held-out
Bengali mathematical reasoning: four with deep Bengali pretraining (TituLLM and
TigerLLM) and three with only incidental Bengali coverage (Qwen3). Each is then
fine-tuned with LoRA on 873 curated Bengali reasoning traces, selected in the
LIMO difficulty window and decontaminated against three public Bengali
mathematics benchmarks, and re-evaluated on the identical items so that every
comparison is paired. Scale predicts performance and pretraining depth does
not. Accuracy rises from 3.6\% to 16.8\% to 44.8\% across the Qwen3 ladder,
while the Bengali-specialised models stay between 0.4\% and 4.4\% across a
threefold parameter range. A 1.7B model with no Bengali continued pretraining
outscores a 3.2B model that has it, at every difficulty tier. Minimal-data
elicitation moves no model significantly, and a control experiment rules out
difficulty miscalibration as the explanation: training on easy, well-matched
problems is significantly worse than training on hard ones. Fine-tuning did
reshape output format, length and stopping behaviour without improving
correctness, which we read as a negative-case instance of the Superficial
Alignment Hypothesis. We also quantify a Bengali tokenizer tax of roughly
$3.1\times$ and show it is separable from reasoning ability: the two most
token-efficient models here are the two weakest at mathematics.
\end{abstract}

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
\item We separate parameter scale from targeted Bengali pretraining depth,
      which prior Bengali model releases confound, and show that scale
      accounts for the variation in mathematical reasoning while depth does
      not. The clearest case is a 1.7B model with no Bengali continued
      pretraining outscoring a 3.2B model that has it, 16.8\% against 1.2\%,
      at easy, medium, hard and olympiad difficulty alike.

\item We report the first paired before-and-after test of LIMO-style
      minimal-data elicitation in Bengali, across seven backbones on identical
      held-out items. No model moves significantly. We argue this bounds the
      claim to the sub-4B range rather than refuting the method, since every
      model here sits below the scale at which arithmetic competence is
      reported to emerge.

\item We rule out the most natural objection to that null with a controlled
      experiment. Holding backbone, adapter configuration, seed and evaluation
      items fixed, training on easy, difficulty-matched problems performs
      significantly worse than training on hard ones, so the null does not
      follow from having selected data that was too difficult.

\item We show that the fine-tuning was not inert even where accuracy did not
      move. It reshaped format, output length and stopping behaviour, most
      starkly in a model that never emitted a stop token before tuning and did
      so on more than half of items afterwards while its accuracy fell. We read
      this as the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis observed in a setting where
      there was little underlying capability to expose.

\item We measure a Bengali tokenizer tax of roughly $3.1\times$ on identical
      text, document the hardware ceilings it imposes on a single 16GB
      consumer card, and show that token efficiency and reasoning ability are
      separable properties rather than a single axis of Bengali competence.

\item We release the full pipeline: the curation and decontamination code, the
      training and evaluation harness, the shared answer scorer with its test
      suite, and the analysis scripts that regenerate every number and figure
      in this paper from the stored model outputs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Statement on AI Assistance}

Anthropic's Claude was used as a coding and writing assistant throughout this
project. Its contributions were: implementing and debugging the training and
evaluation harness, including the LoRA training script, the evaluation loop,
the shared answer scorer and the statistical comparison scripts; diagnosing
several implementation faults, among them a tokenizer and embedding mismatch
that crashed evaluation of one model, and two defects in answer extraction, one
of which had been silently scoring correct arithmetic as incorrect; generating
the figures; and drafting and editing the prose of this manuscript.

The research questions, the experimental design, the data curation pipeline and
the selection of models and datasets are the authors' own. Every number
reported here was produced by scripts in the project repository, and outputs
were checked against raw model completions rather than accepted from summary
statistics alone. No data were generated, altered or imputed by an AI system: every number
reported here comes from a model checkpoint evaluated on stored held-out items,
and no finding, citation or result was produced by the assistant without a
corresponding computation in the repository. Interpretation of the results,
including the decision about what the null result does and does not license, is
the authors' own. The authors reviewed all AI-assisted text and code, and take
full responsibility for the content of this paper, including any remaining
errors.
